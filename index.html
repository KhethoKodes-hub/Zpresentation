Mental health 101

What is mental health?

Mental health is not just the absence of illness. It includes emotional, psychological, social, and spiritual wellbeing.

Just as our bodies can become ill, so can our minds. Anxiety, burnout, grief, and depression are real challenges, not weaknesses or failures.

A healthy mind is not someone who never struggles. It is someone who knows how to rest, seek help, and recover with support.
Everyday stress vs serious struggle
Everyday stress
  • Tension before deadlines
  • Financial pressure
  • Family conflict
  • Temporary worry
Ongoing mental health concerns
  • Persistent sadness
  • Excessive fear
  • Difficulty functioning
  • Sleep, appetite, concentration problems
Warning signs

Signs to watch for

Stress
  • Sleep disturbance
  • Irritability or mood swings
  • Headaches, muscle tension, fatigue
  • Difficulty concentrating
  • Feeling overwhelmed and on edge
Anxiety
  • Racing heart and panic
  • Dizziness or shortness of breath
  • Fear of losing control
  • Restlessness and constant worry
  • Withdrawal from daily life
Depression
  • Persistent sadness or hopelessness
  • Low energy and poor motivation
  • Changes in sleep or eating
  • Feeling like “what is the point?”
  • Thoughts of death or suicide
Burnout
  • Emotional exhaustion
  • Feeling numb or cynical
  • Less empathy and patience
  • Inability to recover
  • Need for rest and boundaries
Biblical perspective

Faith does not eliminate pain

Many faithful people in Scripture experienced grief, fear, exhaustion, and despair.

  • Elijah felt overwhelmed and exhausted.
  • David cried out in many Psalms during deep sorrow.
  • Job suffered unimaginable loss.
  • Jeremiah experienced deep grief and lament.
  • Jesus Himself wrestled in Gethsemane.
Key truths

“Come to me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest.” — Matthew 11:28

God does not reject people because they struggle emotionally. Seeking help is not a lack of faith; it is wisdom and stewardship.

Faith can bring comfort, but it does not replace professional support when someone is in crisis or struggling deeply.
Practical response

What to do in a crisis

ASK

Ask direct, caring questions: “How are you really doing?” “Are you thinking about hurting yourself?”

LISTEN

Listen without judgment. Do not interrupt, argue, or shame.

CONNECT

Stay with the person. Call a trusted person, professional support, family member, pastor, counsellor, or crisis line.

Immediate steps
  • Remove access to means of harm if the risk is severe.
  • Encourage professional help immediately.
  • Contact a doctor, clinic, hospital, or helpline.
  • Do not leave someone alone if immediate danger is a concern.
  • Offer hope, presence, and practical support.
When someone is in crisis, calm presence and urgent action matter more than the perfect words.
Self-care & coping

Healthy ways to care for yourself

Daily check-ins
  • How am I feeling right now?
  • Have I eaten, slept, or rested?
  • What is taking up most of my headspace?
  • What do I need today?
Stress reduction
  • Breathing exercises
  • Walking and movement
  • Prayer and reflection
  • Journaling and rest
  • Time with trusted people
Self-care
  • Eat well and sleep enough
  • Exercise regularly
  • Take breaks and set boundaries
  • Practice good hygiene
  • Reduce harmful coping habits

Closing message

You are not weak for needing help

Seeking help is not failure. It is wisdom.

Being open about mental health within the church builds trust, reduces shame, and creates a safer place for everyone.

Let the church be a place where people can say: “I am struggling,” and be met with compassion, prayer, and practical support.
Simple takeaway
  • Notice what is happening in your mind and body.
  • Talk early instead of hiding.
  • Use faith and professional help together.
  • Support others with patience and humility.
  • Remember: caring for your mind is part of caring for your whole self.
